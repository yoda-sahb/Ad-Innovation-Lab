# The Ad Innovation Lab Method

## Start with the problem that survives the roadmap

The lab is designed for structural advertising problems, not feature ideation.

A useful problem usually has several properties:

- it survives multiple product cycles;
- multiple teams have learned to work around it;
- the same failure keeps returning under different names;
- solving it creates reusable capability rather than a one-off feature;
- the obvious answers have already been tried; and
- the economics are material enough to justify changing the system.

## The sequence

### 1. Possibility
State the structural problem or opportunity in one sentence.

Do not start with a solution name.

### 2. Mechanism
Identify the smallest causal change that could alter the outcome.

The mechanism should make clear:

- who owns the decision;
- what information is required;
- what rights and constraints remain intact;
- what changes in the system;
- what explicitly does not change.

### 3. Proof
Define the smallest credible experiment.

At minimum:

- baseline;
- treatment;
- primary metric;
- guardrails;
- evidence / causality method;
- time window;
- operational constraints.

### 4. Kill test
Write the result that causes the lab to stop, narrow, or reject the thesis.

If the idea cannot be killed, it is not yet research.

### 5. Prototype
Build only enough artifact to answer a real uncertainty.

A prototype can test feasibility, semantics, workflow, latency, architecture, or participant behavior. It cannot by itself prove causal business value.

### 6. Evidence
Separate:

- documented external evidence;
- strategic interpretation;
- working hypothesis;
- prototype behavior;
- measured causal result.

Never collapse these into “proven.”

### 7. Commit or kill
The outcome is one of three decisions:

- **BUILD** — the mechanism created material value and the system change is justified;
- **NARROW** — part of the thesis worked, but the architecture is larger than necessary;
- **KILL** — the current system is good enough, the mechanism failed, or the economics do not justify the change.

## The anti-patterns

### Architecture-first innovation
A large architecture is designed before anyone proves the root problem cannot be solved inside the current system.

### Demo theater
A prototype is treated as evidence that customers, economics, or market structure will behave as assumed.

### AI wallpaper
A model or agent is inserted into an existing workflow without identifying a new system-level advantage.

### Standard before value
The industry is asked to coordinate before one participant has demonstrated enough value to justify coordination.

### The elephant anti-pattern
A strategy assumes another actor will voluntarily stop spending, surrender economics, abandon an incentive, cooperate, or behave “rationally” merely because the proposed system is globally more efficient.

Design around incentives that actually exist.

## Executive output

For a real business problem, the ideal lab output is one page:

1. **Root problem**
2. **System layer**
3. **Business invariants**
4. **Mechanism**
5. **90-day proof**
6. **Kill condition**
7. **Decision owner**
8. **Build / narrow / kill**

The goal is not to make innovation look sophisticated.

The goal is to make the decision obvious enough that a company can act on it.
